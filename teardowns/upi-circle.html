💸 PM Teardown #1

UPI Circle x Pocket Money: A PM Teardown

NPCI built the rails for inclusion. GPay built a product for the mainstream. This teardown is about the gap between those two goals, and who falls through it.

UPI NPCI Fintech Product Thinking Payments Delegated Payments

Problem

The Problem

I used to book Rapido rides for my parents because they never went digital. I'd pay, they'd travel. It worked, but I was always the middleman, a one-person bridge between their offline life and a digital payment. Separately, I'd sometimes ask my cook to pick up groceries I missed ordering. She'd pay from her own pocket, I'd reimburse her later, and neither of us was comfortable with the arrangement. These are the two exact problems UPI Circle was built to solve. Most teardowns start with the feature. This one starts with the people who needed it before it existed. I work in payments investigations at HSBC. I see what happens when trust breaks down in digital transactions. That context shapes everything in this teardown.

👵
The digitally-excluded parent

Kavitha, 63, retired schoolteacher. Tried UPI once at a pharmacy, entered her PIN wrong twice, panicked, and paid cash. Now she keeps ₹3,000 in her purse at all times. Her son books Rapido for her, pays her medical bills online, and calls the electricity company when there's an issue. She's not incapable, she's anxious about making an irreversible mistake with money.

Workaround: Her son is the real user of every digital service she consumes. She's a dependent on someone else's digital life.
🍳
The trusted household help

Savitha cooks and cleans for three families in Bangalore. For one of them, she sometimes picks up vegetables or household items from the market on the way. The employer hands her ₹200-300 in the morning, guessing what might be needed. Savitha sometimes pays from her own pocket and gets reimbursed. Sometimes not. There's no accountability either way, the employer doesn't know what was spent, and Savitha isn't always comfortable asking.

Workaround: Cash handovers based on guesswork. No record, no control, and no dignity in the arrangement.
🎓
The college student away from home

Riya is in her second year of engineering in Pune. Her parents are in Chennai. They transfer money monthly and she manages it herself, sometimes too well, sometimes not. They have no visibility into whether she's eating properly. She won't ask for more money mid-month because she doesn't want them to worry. They can't top her up silently for a specific need without it feeling like a check-in.

Workaround: Monthly lump-sum transfers. No visibility for parents, no frictionless top-up for genuine needs.
The same thread runs through all three: I want to enable someone I trust, without handing them full control. I want to help, without becoming the middleman every time.

Feature

What Is UPI Circle

UPI Circle lets a primary user (account holder) delegate spending power to a secondary user, without giving them full account access. Think of it as digital pocket money, with controls cash never had.

Mode Who transacts Best for
Full delegation Secondary user transacts within a preset monthly limit Child, elderly parent
Partial delegation Primary user approves and authorizes each transaction Maid, new staff

Live on GPay, PhonePe, Paytm and BHIM since August 2024.

What the spec doesn't capture is why this took so long. UPI has been live since 2016. The delegated payments problem, I want someone I trust to spend from my account without giving them my PIN, has existed since day one. UPI Circle launched in 2024. Eight years of workarounds for a problem that was visible from the start.

Consumer Layer

GPay's Pocket Money: The Consumer Layer

NPCI doesn't build consumer products. It publishes a spec and lets apps implement it. GPay was the first to build something consumers actually recognize, they called it Pocket Money, gave it a dedicated UI, and made it feel like a feature rather than a protocol. That distinction matters.

🏷️ Branding: "Pocket Money"
What GPay added A name that lands immediately. "UPI Circle" asks users to understand a protocol. "Pocket Money" asks them to remember a childhood concept. GPay chose to frame the feature around a familiar real-world behavior instead of a technical one.
What it reveals Naming is positioning. "Pocket Money" signals this is for families, not enterprises. It narrows the use case but deepens relevance for the mainstream audience GPay is chasing.
🔗 Relationship tagging
What GPay added GPay asks you to label the secondary user, parent, child, spouse, household help. NPCI's spec doesn't require this. GPay uses it to pre-configure sensible defaults and make the trust relationship legible at a glance in the UI.
What it reveals The relationship label is context that makes the product smarter. It's also data. GPay is building a graph of trust relationships at scale, which has obvious value beyond this feature.
🔔 Notification design as the control experience
What GPay added Primary users get a push notification for every secondary user transaction, with the merchant name and amount. This isn't just an alert, it is the control experience. The notification is what turns delegation from "I gave them access" into "I can see exactly what they're doing."
What it reveals Visibility is what makes trust scalable. Without notifications, delegation feels like losing control. With them, it feels like extending it. GPay understood that the primary user's confidence is the product's core retention metric.

JTBD

Jobs to Be Done

👵 Digitally-excluded parent

"When I need to get somewhere and my child has already booked the Rapido, I want to pay at the destination myself, so my child doesn't have to be on call for money or wait for a transfer to reflect before I can leave."

🎓 College student away from home

"When I need to pay for something urgent and my account balance is low, I want to use my parent's pre-authorized limit, so I don't have to call them mid-day and wait for a transfer to reflect."

🍳 Cook / household help

"When my employer asks me to pick up groceries on the way, I want to pay from a pre-authorized amount, so I'm never spending my own money on someone else's needs and never have to ask for reimbursement."

Key Insight

The first two JTBDs are written from the secondary user's perspective, but the real anxiety being solved belongs to the primary user: the child who is always on call, the parent who is always waiting to transfer. The third JTBD flips the subject entirely. For the cook persona, the job is as much about the secondary user's dignity as it is about the primary user's control. UPI Circle solves both anxieties, but only the third one makes it visible.

Design Decisions

Design Decisions

Five deliberate decisions in the NPCI spec, and one more that GPay made on its own.

🔐 Biometric mandatory for secondary users
Decision Biometric or app password is mandatory for every secondary user before any transaction, not equally enforced for primary users.
Reason Primary users own their device and are trusted by default. Secondary users operate on devices the primary has no control over. Without biometric enforcement, anyone who picks up the secondary user's phone can transact freely, something regular UPI doesn't protect against in a delegated model.
Tradeoff Elderly and low-income users (the exact personas UPI Circle is designed to include) are most likely to have older, non-biometric devices. Good security decision. Incomplete inclusivity solution.
💰 ₹15,000/month spending cap
Decision Monthly spending limit for secondary users is capped at ₹15,000, with a per-transaction cap of ₹5,000.
Reason UPI Circle expands one bank account across up to 5 devices. Each additional device is a new attack surface. The cap limits the blast radius: the maximum financial damage possible if a secondary user's device is stolen or compromised.
Tradeoff Elderly parents managing medical expenses and full-time caregivers handling household needs regularly exceed ₹15,000/month, forcing a cash fallback for the excess. The cap controls fraud but caps genuine utility.
👤 One primary user per secondary user
Decision Each secondary user can be linked to only one primary user at a time. Primary users can have up to 5 secondary users.
Reason If a secondary user could be added to multiple circles simultaneously, there would be no clean way to determine which account was debited for a given transaction, making dispute resolution and liability assignment impossible.
Tradeoff Multi-funder use cases (both parents independently supporting one child) are not natively supported. One parent must transfer money to the other first, adding friction to a legitimate use case.
📋 Two trust gates at setup
Decision Secondary users can only be added via the primary user's contact list or QR scan, never by manually typing a phone number. The primary user must enter their UPI PIN during the setup/linking process itself.
Reason The contact list requirement protects the secondary user. If anyone could type any number, a stranger could unknowingly be enrolled in someone's circle and potentially used as a mule account. The PIN at setup protects the primary user. It confirms the account holder is the one creating the circle, not someone who borrowed or stole their phone.
Tradeoff Remote onboarding is impossible. Adding an elderly parent in another city requires them to generate and share a QR code in person, creating friction for the very use case the feature is designed for.
⏰ 24-hour cool-off period
Decision After linking a new secondary user, transactions are capped at ₹5,000 for the first 24 hours regardless of the full delegation limit.
Reason The highest fraud risk in any delegated system is the moment of onboarding, before the primary user has observed the secondary user transact even once. The cool-off period buys time to catch a mistaken or fraudulent addition before it can be exploited at scale.
Tradeoff Adding an elderly parent right before a hospital visit means they can only transact ₹5,000 in the first 24 hours, which may be insufficient for medical expenses. Security chosen deliberately over convenience at onboarding.
🪪 GPay's KYC requirement for secondary users
Decision GPay requires government ID verification (Aadhaar or PAN) for secondary users during Pocket Money setup. NPCI's UPI Circle spec does not mandate this. It is GPay's own fraud mitigation layer on top of the protocol.
Reason Secondary users operate on devices GPay doesn't control. KYC ties the secondary account to a verified real-world identity, reducing the risk of a fraudulent secondary user being added, a scammer posing as household help, or a stranger enrolled without the secondary user's knowledge.
Tradeoff This is the most friction-heavy step in the entire setup flow, and it lands on the users least equipped to handle it, elderly parents who may not have their Aadhaar card handy, and household help who may not have a government ID in the required format. GPay's fraud prevention logic is sound. But it partially excludes the exact personas UPI Circle was designed to serve.

Metrics

How I'd Measure Success as PM

📈 Adoption
  • % of active UPI users with at least one UPI Circle set up, tracked weekly post launch
🔄 Engagement
  • Avg transactions per secondary user per month
  • % of circles with at least one transaction in last 30 days
🎯 Outcome
  • Reduction in P2P transfers from primary to secondary after circle setup (proxy for cash displacement)
  • % of secondary users who were previously unbanked
🚨 Guardrail
  • % of transactions disputed as unauthorized
  • % of circles revoked within 7 days of setup (fraud or trust breakdown at onboarding)
  • Avg time to dispute resolution for unauthorized UPI Circle transactions
  • % of disputed transactions where secondary user was added within last 7 days (onboarding-stage fraud flag)
💚 Health
  • % of secondary users who complete their first transaction within 7 days of setup (activation rate, a circle that never transacts is a setup that failed its job)
  • % of full-delegation circles that switch to partial delegation within 30 days (signals the primary user lost confidence in the secondary user, worth investigating whether this is a product problem or a relationship problem)

What I'd Add

Three things to make it a much better product.

1 Relationship-based dynamic caps
Gap The ₹15,000 monthly cap is a blunt instrument. A parent funding a college student's living expenses has a different trust profile, spending pattern, and risk exposure than an employer authorizing a cook to buy vegetables. One flat ceiling for every relationship type in India is not a product decision. It is an absence of one.
Fix Set the default cap by relationship type at onboarding: higher for immediate family (parent, spouse, child), lower for household staff and friends, with the primary user able to adjust within a band defined by their bank. The cap becomes a starting point calibrated to the relationship, not a universal ceiling imposed on it.
Outcome Primary users can genuinely support dependents with real-world expenses without hitting an arbitrary limit. Fraud exposure stays controlled because the band is set by the bank, not by the user alone.
2 Lightweight re-onboarding for elderly users who fail KYC
Gap GPay's KYC step requires government ID verification that elderly users and household help may not have readily available. When setup fails at this step, there is no guided fallback, the user is simply blocked, with no path forward. The feature designed to include them ends up excluding them at setup.
Fix Offer Aadhaar OTP-based verification as an alternative KYC path. Most elderly users have an Aadhaar number even if they don't have a physical card or a PAN. An OTP to their registered mobile number is a lighter, more accessible identity confirmation that still satisfies KYC requirements.
Outcome Pocket Money becomes accessible to the elderly parent and household help personas it was designed for, without compromising GPay's fraud prevention. Aadhaar OTP is still KYC, it's just lower-friction KYC. The feature finally serves the people NPCI built the rails for.
3 Close the cash fallback loop for bill payments and recharges
Gap Caregivers cannot pay electricity bills or mobile recharges for dependents through UPI Circle, still falling back to cash for these needs, which partially defeats the feature's purpose.
Fix Introduce escrow-style settlement for irreversible payment types, funds held briefly before release, to safely enable bill payments without the fraud risk that comes with instant, irreversible transactions.
Outcome Caregivers can manage the full spectrum of dependent needs digitally, completing the financial inclusion promise of UPI Circle.
"NPCI built UPI Circle for inclusion. GPay built Pocket Money for mainstream adoption. These are not the same goal, and the gap shows up exactly where it hurts, in the KYC step that blocks the elderly parent, in the QR code you have to generate and share just to add an elderly parent who lives in another city, in the notification that feels like control to the primary user and surveillance to the secondary one. I book Rapido rides for my parents because they never went digital. UPI Circle is the right answer to that problem. Pocket Money is a good first product on top of it. But the person it was built for is still waiting at the KYC screen."
